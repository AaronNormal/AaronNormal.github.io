Which dating apps actually show people near you?

‘Nearby’ is the most trusted number in dating, and the least reliable. Here’s how location really works on the apps, why ‘2 miles away’ is often a fiction, and what ‘accurate’ should mean when you actually want to meet tonight.

The Everl team

‘Nearby’ is the number we trust most, and check least

Open any dating app and the first thing you read after a face is a distance: 2 miles away, 500 metres away, active now. It feels like a fact: a small, precise, reassuring number that says this is real, this is close, this could actually happen tonight. It’s also the number almost nobody questions.

Which is strange, because it’s a guess. The distance on a dating app isn’t a measurement of where someone is right now: it’s an estimate built from a location reading that might be coarse, cached, or deliberately set somewhere else. Once you know how it’s made, ‘2 miles away’ stops being a promise and starts being marketing.

The three ways ‘nearby’ lies

First, GPS is fuzzy on purpose. Apps coarsen your location for privacy, it drifts badly indoors and between tall buildings, and it’s frequently cached. So the reading can be from where you were an hour ago, not where you are now. A ‘1 mile away’ can be a phone sitting on a desk across town.

Second, on several apps you can simply choose to be somewhere else. Paid ‘passport’ or ‘travel’ modes let people set their location to another city entirely: great for planning a trip, useless for trusting that the person in your feed is actually near you tonight. And even honest accounts show a distance from wherever the app last had a fix.

Third, there’s the lag between ‘was here’ and ‘is here’. Some apps are built on crossed paths. They show you people whose route overlapped yours at some point, which is a lovely idea and a historical one: it tells you where someone was, not that they’re a two-minute walk away right now. ‘Near’ and ‘now’ are two different claims, and most apps only really make the first.

So what should ‘accurate’ actually mean?

Here’s the honest reframe: accuracy on a meeting app was never about a prettier distance number. A tighter GPS reading is still a reading: still an estimate of a position, still fakeable, still possibly stale. The only thing that actually matters when you want to meet someone is simpler and harder to fake: are the two of you genuinely in the same place?

That’s a different kind of proof. It’s the difference between a map saying you’re close and your two phones confirming it, because they can only ‘hear’ each other when they really are within a few metres. Short-range radio doesn’t reach across a city. If it connects, you’re in the same bar, the same carriage, the same short walk. That’s not an estimate; it’s confirmation.

How the apps handle ‘near me’

Most swipe apps show a GPS distance, and some let paying users set a different location. It’s fine for browsing, but ‘nearby’ is an editable estimate, and you’ll typically message for days before anyone’s actually in the same room. Happn is built on crossed paths, so it’s genuinely about proximity, but historical proximity, where you were, not a live ‘they’re here now’. Map-style apps drop people as dots you approach, which is closer to real-time but is still a GPS dot, and putting yourself on a map to be approached isn’t for everyone.

How to read a distance number like someone who knows

Until every app proves co-presence, you’re reading estimates. So read them properly. Treat the distance as a range, not a point: a ‘2 miles’ is somewhere between ‘genuinely round the corner’ and ‘a stale fix from this morning’, and the app won’t tell you which. Pair it with the last-active time, which is usually the more honest of the two numbers; a tight distance with a vague ‘active today’ is a weaker signal than a fuzzy distance with ‘active now’.

Then test it early rather than late. The cheapest way to find out whether someone is actually nearby and actually free is to suggest something small and soon (a coffee, a drink, this week) and see what happens. People genuinely in your city say yes or offer an alternative; people whose location is aspirational tend to keep the conversation in the app. You’ll learn more from one low-stakes suggestion than from three days of messaging, and you’ll lose nothing but the messaging.

Whatever the number says, meet safely

Proximity is convenience, not vetting. Someone being confirmed as three metres away tells you where they are: it tells you nothing about who they are, and no technology on any app changes that. The basics don’t stop applying because the meeting happened quickly.

So keep them: meet somewhere public and reasonably busy the first time, tell a friend where you’re going and roughly when you’ll be back, arrange your own way there and back, and leave whenever you want to, without needing a reason. Trust the feeling if something’s off: a good meeting survives you being sensible about it, and anyone who objects to any of the above has told you something useful.

For a date night, this is the whole game

Think about what you actually want on a spontaneous night out. Not a match sitting at home across the city who you’ll trade messages with until the moment passes: someone who is genuinely out, genuinely near, and up for the same thing right now. On a date-night timescale, a distance estimate is worthless and a confirmed ‘we’re in the same place’ is everything.

That’s the bar worth holding apps to. Not ‘how close does it say they are’, but ‘can it prove they’re actually here’. When meeting tonight is the point, confirmed beats nearby every time, and confirmed is the only kind of accuracy that ever mattered.

Common questions

Can you fake your location on dating apps?

On many, yes: paid ‘passport’ or ‘travel’ features let people set their location to another city, and even ordinary GPS can be coarse or hours out of date. That’s why the ‘nearby’ distance isn’t proof that someone is actually close right now.

The honest answer is that GPS-based apps all estimate distance, so none are truly reliable for ‘right now’. Short-range checks help: as you get close, Everl can use both phones’ Bluetooth to check you’re in the same place.

How do I stay safe meeting someone nearby?

Proximity tells you where someone is, not who they are, so the usual rules still apply: meet somewhere public and busy the first time, tell a friend where you’ll be and when you’re due back, sort your own travel both ways, and leave whenever you want without needing a reason.
