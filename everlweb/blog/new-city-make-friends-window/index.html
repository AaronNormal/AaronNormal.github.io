Moved to a new city? Your first months count most

There’s a quiet, time-limited advantage to being new in a city, and most people waste it waiting to feel settled. Here’s how to use your window before it closes.

The Everl team

The window is real, and it closes

For roughly the first six to twelve months, you have a social advantage. You’re the new person. Invitations flow more freely, colleagues include you deliberately, and "I’ve just moved here" is a complete and socially acceptable explanation for wanting to meet people. Nobody finds it odd. Later, the same sentence needs a preamble.

To be straight with you, the six-month figure is a rule of thumb rather than something measured in a lab: the useful truth behind it is that the advantage is real and it decays. After a while everyone’s routines re-solidify, you stop being new, and the doors get quietly heavier. The people who build a real circle in a new city are usually just the ones who moved while it was easy instead of waiting to feel ready.

Which is unfortunate, because waiting is exactly what most people do. The first months are when you’re most tired, least settled and least inclined to go out, and also when going out pays the highest return you’ll ever get in that city.

Week one: build the skeleton

Before you meet a single person, decide where you’re going to be a regular. Pick a café, a gym or pool, a running route, a pub, a shop. Go at roughly the same times. This sounds like it has nothing to do with friendship, and it’s the foundation of all of it: you’re rebuilding the ambient layer that your old city gave you for free.

Then find one recurring thing with other humans in it and book yourself in before the enthusiasm fades: a class, a club, a team, a volunteering slot. One is plenty. A single weekly commitment will produce more familiar faces in two months than any amount of one-off events, because the same people keep coming back.

And say yes to the work thing. Every single work thing, early on. Colleagues are the highest-yield social source most movers have and the one people most often write off as "not real friends", which is a judgement you’re not equipped to make in week one, and which conveniently excuses you from going.

Your only job is to say yes

In the early months, treat every invitation as a yes by default. The drink, the flat-warming, the "a few of us are heading to…". You’ll be knackered, you won’t know anyone, the flat will be full of boxes, and staying in will feel entirely justified. Go anyway.

Momentum in a new city is built almost entirely out of showing up to things you didn’t organise. And invitations are self-reinforcing in both directions: people who come get asked again, people who decline twice quietly stop being asked. That’s not anyone being cruel: it’s just how invitation lists work. You can be tired later, once you have people to be tired with.

The second-invite rule

Here’s where most new-city social lives stall, and it’s oddly specific. You meet someone, you get on, you both say "we should do this again", and then neither of you organises it. Everyone leaves it hanging, assuming the other person will pick it up, and a promising acquaintance evaporates for no reason at all.

So adopt a rule: you send the second invitation. Not the vague kind: a specific one, within a fortnight, tied to something concrete. "There’s a thing on at the Whitworth Saturday, fancy it?" "Pub Thursday?" You will feel briefly like you’re being forward. You are not. You’re being the only person in the situation doing the obvious thing, and it’s the single highest-leverage habit for building a circle from zero.

Don’t put it all on the first few people

The classic new-city mistake is loading your entire social hope onto the first two people who are nice to you. It’s a lot of pressure on them and on you, and it tends to end with disappointment on both sides when they turn out to have a full life already.

Aim for a spread instead: the gym person, the work friend, the neighbour, the run-club regular, the friend-of-a-friend you were introduced to. A wide, shallow network is more robust than a narrow deep one at this stage, and it converts over time: some of those acquaintances become real friends, and you can’t predict which, so you need several in play.

The month-three dip is normal

Expect a bad patch somewhere around the second or third month. The novelty has worn off, the admin is done, you know a reasonable number of people to nod at and nobody yet to call at 9pm on a Tuesday. It’s the point where everything feels like effort and the move starts to look like a mistake.

This is not a signal that it isn’t working. It’s what the middle of the process feels like, because acquaintances take months of repetition to become friends and you’re standing in the middle of the repetition. The specific danger of this stage is that it’s exactly when people quietly stop going to the thing they’d committed to: three weeks before it would have paid off.

So plan for it now: whatever your one recurring commitment is, decide in advance that you’ll keep going through the dip. Message home when you need to, and keep turning up. It lifts, usually without you noticing the moment it does.

Be the one who asks, and who’s already out

Take the initiative most people are too shy to take: ask for the number, send the "good to meet you, let’s do it again." It feels exposing precisely because everyone is waiting for someone else to do it, which is also why it works so reliably.

And for the in-between nights (new in town, nothing on, but you’d happily meet someone if it were easy) that’s the gap proximity fills. Stay consistent and the early, invisible work starts to cluster. A city goes from strangers to yours faster than you’d think, but only if you move while the window’s open.

Common questions

How long does it take to make friends in a new city?

Expect a few months of repeated contact before acquaintances become friends, and use the first 6-12 months while you’re still "the new person" and invitations come easily. That window is a rule of thumb rather than a hard number, but the advantage is real and it fades, so start saying yes early rather than waiting to feel settled.

How do I meet people after moving somewhere new?

Become a regular somewhere in week one, commit to one recurring activity, say yes to every work invitation early, and always send the second invitation yourself. Spread your hope across several people rather than one or two, since you can’t tell yet which acquaintances will become friends.

Why am I so lonely after moving to a new city?

Because you lost every built-in social structure overnight and have to rebuild from zero: that’s normal and temporary, not a reflection on you. Most people also hit a dip around month two or three, when the novelty has gone but nobody is close yet; it’s the middle of the process, not evidence it’s failing.

What should I do in my first week in a new city?

Pick the places you’ll be a regular at (a café, a gym, a route, a pub) and start going at consistent times. Then book one recurring group activity before the enthusiasm fades. You’re rebuilding the ambient familiarity your old city gave you for free, and it needs to be deliberate now.
